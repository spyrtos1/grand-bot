## 1. Title

**Seam** *(La Suture)*

---

## 2. Concept text — 180 words max (submitted with NO designer name attached)

> Seam is a walked geography. Cyprus, where I was born, is cut by a line that appears on
> no map but shapes every daily gesture. Carrara, where I have worked for years, is a
> mountain quarried open: its geography is a wound that becomes marble, then monument,
> then export. Alternative Geographies begins, for me, with these two cuts.
>
> The garden is a low spine of Gaspésie fieldstone and cedar running the full length of a
> 10 × 20 m plot, splitting it into two paths that run parallel and never quite reconnect.
> At intervals along the spine, blocks of pale Carrara marble are grafted into the darker
> local stone — carried across an ocean the same way quarry lines, shipping lines and
> border lines already carry material and consequence across the world.
>
> Visitors choose a side and walk the length of the garden. At the far end, the two paths
> meet again around a single worked stone, cut low enough to sit on. The line that
> divides becomes, for a moment, a bench: two people at the same height, facing the same
> river.

(177 words — under the 180-word cap.)

## 3. Suggested plant list

This is a hardscape-led garden (stone spine, minimal planting), so the list is deliberately
short — plants read as what colonizes a seam over time, not as a flower bed. Zone 4b
(Grand-Métis), per the guidelines.

- Low native fescue (*Festuca* spp.) and hair grass (*Deschampsia flexuosa*) along the
  open, sunnier edge of the spine
- Creeping thyme in the dry joints between fieldstone, where foot traffic allows
- Transplanted moss on the north-facing stone faces, in the plot's shadier half
- Wood fern (*Dryopteris*) in the deepest joint, near the meeting-point bench

*(A festival horticulturalist should sanity-check availability/hardiness before this goes
into the submission — they grow most stock from their own greenhouses starting in March.)*

## 4. Layout plan, elevations, perspective — NOT DRAFTED HERE

The call requires, as two separate 11″×17″/A3 panels:
- A layout plan at minimum 1:100 scale, plus the plant list above
- Two main elevations (any scale) and a perspective/sketch/rendering showing the whole
  garden

These need an actual scaled drawing. Suggested next step: take the construction notes
below to whoever drafts for you (or sketch it yourself), specifying:
- Plot: 10 m × 20 m, single entry/exit from either the west or east edge (confirm which
  with the festival once a site is assigned)
- Spine: a single dry-stacked stone-and-timber ridge running the full 20 m length,
  roughly 60 cm wide at the base, tapering to ~30 cm at a walkable top edge, height
  varying 40–70 cm
- Two parallel paths flank the spine, each wide enough for one person
- 3–5 marble insertions along the spine's length, each roughly 20–40 cm long, flush with
  the stone face — NOT protruding, so they read as "grafted in" rather than applied
- At the far end (opposite the entry), the spine widens and drops to seat height (~45 cm),
  forming the shared bench where both paths converge

### 70-word answer: "How does your project tackle Alternative Geographies?"

> Seam makes visible two geographies usually kept separate: the political line dividing
> Cyprus, and the quarry line opening Carrara. Neither appears on a normal map, yet both
> organize movement, ownership, belonging. Grafting carried Carrara marble into Gaspésie
> fieldstone turns an invisible, structural border into a walkable, touchable seam — then
> turns that seam into a shared bench, where division briefly becomes a meeting point.

## 7. End-of-life / durability note

The 2027 call requires a strategy for repurposing or recycling materials after the
garden's run (minimum two summers on site). Because the spine is dry-stacked (no
mortar), this is straightforward to write honestly:
- Fieldstone: fully redistributable elsewhere on the festival grounds, or returned to
  source
- Cedar: reclaimed or composted
- The 3–5 marble insertions: small enough to be removed intact and returned to the
  artist's studio for reuse in future work — genuinely closing the "carried" material's
  loop rather than discarding it
